Part I · L01 · Pre-class reading

The cold brick — and the two pillars

L01 Pre-class · Step 1 / 10How this works, and the two pillars

Ten short steps, each deeper than the last. By the end you can: tell the cold-brick story twice (macro & micro); distinguish temperature, heat, and thermal equilibrium; explain why thermodynamics is possible at all; and say what L01 deliberately does not contain.

Pillar 1 — Thermodynamics (first half): macroscopic variables (T, V, p), thermal expansion, phase change, heat and its transfer methods, equilibrium. Built on averages over ~10²³ particles.
Pillar 2 — Oscillations & Waves (second half): collective motion of the same molecules — guitar strings, sound, ocean waves. Described by a wave equation; again no per-particle tracking.
⚠️ L01 contains no formulas. If you catch yourself deriving one here, you're ahead of the lecture — park it.

L01 Pre-class · Step 2 / 10The cold brick — predict first

September 9, Hebb building. A brick out of a cooler. First guess: liquid nitrogen. Wrong — dry ice, so it starts at about −78.5 °C, then sits in the ~20 °C room.

Predict, then check against the slides — in the first minute, what do you actually see?

(source + guided physical inference: the photos show what happened; the why is labelled inference, not lecture content)

✗ A · Nothing — unchanged. Look at the temperature difference: −78.5 °C vs ~20 °C. Something dramatic happens at the surface.
✓ B · Frost forms on the brick's surface. Water vapour in the air touches a surface far below 0 °C and leaves the gas phase → frost forms almost immediately — exactly what the ~15 min photo shows. Heat starts flowing in and the brick begins warming.
✗ C · The brick glows faintly. Glowing usually means visible light — that needs a far higher temperature. The brick IS emitting thermal radiation right now, mainly in the infrared, which your eyes cannot detect.
✗ D · Water pools underneath it. Meltwater only appears once the surface climbs past 0 °C — and the ~1 hr photo shows the frost gone, not water pooling. First comes frost.

What actually happened (slide photos)

  • ~15 min: frost over the whole brick.
  • ~25 min: frost retreating — corners and edges clear first (most exposed surface per amount of brick → they warm fastest).
  • ~1 hr: brick nearly clear, temperature approaching room temperature.
Wording trap: slides say condensation, but at −78.5 °C the vapour goes from gas straight to solid — deposition (凝华). For the quiz, answer with the instructor's wording; keep "deposition" as private precision.

L01 Pre-class · Step 3 / 10The macroscopic view

Macroscopic = the scale you can see or measure. Five measurable statements:

  • Heat flowed in — from everything warmer. (Faster-with-bigger-ΔT is a preview inference — L01 shows the flow, not the rate law.)
  • Its temperature rose — continuously, then more slowly.
  • Its dimensions grew — every linear dimension, slightly (thermal expansion — first real topic).
  • Frost formed, then cleared — a phase change in the air next to the brick.
  • It stopped changing — brick and room at the same temperature: thermal equilibrium.
This language never mentions atoms. That's the point — macroscopic variables are chosen so you don't need them.

L01 Pre-class · Step 4 / 10The microscopic view

  • The brick's atoms vibrate about fixed equilibrium positions (solid — contrast with gas molecules, which travel). Cold brick = slow, low-energy vibrations.
  • Air molecules collide with it — each impact transfers a bit of momentum and energy; billions per second = "heat flowing in".
  • Everything radiates — wavelength set by temperature; room temperature → mainly infrared, invisible. IR-camera demo promised.
Go deeper: individual collisions can transfer energy in either direction. Because the warmer side transfers more on average, there is a net flow from hot to cold. The word doing the work is net.

L01 Pre-class · Step 5 / 10The bridge: averages become variables

microscopic behaviour → averages → macroscopic variables → thermodynamics

  • The brick's size (Volume) is related to the average spacing between atoms — why expansion is visible at all.
  • The brick's temperature ↔ the average kinetic energy of its particles. Prof's phrasing: "the average of all those kinetic-energy values is what we call temperature."
That is Thermodynamics: summarizing 10²³ things into a handful of variables, then finding the rules those summaries obey.

L01 Pre-class · Step 6 / 10Temperature vs Heat vs Equilibrium

Temperature — a property of the object, a state variable (状态量). In the L01 model: the average kinetic energy of its particles.
Heat — energy in transit, driven by a temperature difference; exists only while crossing a boundary; carried by collisions and radiation.
Thermal Equilibrium — endpoint of heat flow: common temperature; the net exchange drops to zero because the averages have matched. (Microscopic interpretation.)
The two traps: ① "the brick contains heat" — wrong: it contains internal energy; heat is energy while moving between things. ② "at equilibrium everything stops" — wrong: only the net flow stops; collisions continue both ways. Equilibrium = balanced traffic, not an empty road.

L01 Pre-class · Step 7 / 10The waves teaser

  • The brick is also hit by sound and light — waves: collective motion, energy travels without the material travelling.
  • Sound waves, guitar strings, ocean waves; transverse and longitudinal oscillations.
  • Same trick as thermodynamics: no per-particle tracking — a wave equation summarizes it.

L01 Pre-class · Step 8 / 10Your concept graph

L01 Pre-class · Step 9 / 10Check yourself

1 · Why is tracking every particle impossible — and why is that fine?
~10²³ particles; the averages are all that matter, and averages appear as macroscopic variables. Summaries + rules = thermodynamics.
2 · How does a brick atom's motion differ from an air molecule's?
Brick atoms vibrate about fixed positions; gas molecules travel long distances between collisions.
3 · Which is the state variable — temperature or heat?
Temperature. Heat is energy in transit, driven by ΔT, carried by collisions and radiation.
4 · At thermal equilibrium, what exactly stops — and what continues?
The net flow stops; individual two-way exchanges continue forever.
5 · Why did the frost clear from corners and edges first?
Most exposed surface per amount of brick → fastest warming. Geometry controls heat-flow rate.
6 · The brick radiates constantly — why can't you see it?
Room-temperature emission is mainly infrared — outside the narrow visible band our eyes detect.

L01 Pre-class · Step 10 / 1060-second review

  • Macroscopic variables = averages over ~10²³ particles.
  • Temperature = average kinetic energy in the L01 model — a state variable.
  • Heat = energy in transit (collisions + radiation); never "stored".
  • Cold brick: heat in → warms → expands; frost deposits, clears corners first.
  • Equilibrium: same T, net flow zero, two-way collisions continue.
  • Everything radiates; room-T = infrared, invisible. Waves = collective motion.

Worked example — the two-brick question

Brick A at 20 °C, Brick B at 100 °C, same room. (a) Do both emit EM radiation? (b) Would the radiation from A and B have exactly the same characteristics? (c) Direction of net heat flow for each? (d) As B cools?

(a)
Yes — both. Every object emits regardless of temperature.
(b)
No. Different temperatures ⇒ different characteristic wavelength/frequency. (L01 stops here — it does not ask which pours out more energy.)
(c)
A: in equilibrium with the room → net flow = 0 (two-way exchanges cancel). B: hotter than the room → net flow from B into the air.
(d)
ΔT shrinks → net flow shrinks → zero once B eventually reaches the same temperature as the room — thermal equilibrium, in the instructor's own words.

Variations

V1 · A very large cool metal block and a tiny hot metal bead touch. Which way does heat flow? Does the larger object's greater total internal energy decide it?
Heat flows from the hotter bead to the cooler block. Direction is set by temperature difference, not by size or total energy.
V2 · At the instant of equilibrium, what is zero and what is not?
Zero: net energy flow. Not zero: the individual two-way exchanges — balanced traffic, not an empty road.
V3 · The frost is gone by ~1 hr. What does L01 let you say?
Frost forms on the very cold brick and later disappears as the brick warms. That is the full claim. (Vocabulary: slides say "condensation"; strictly, gas → solid frost is closer to deposition.)

Part III · L02 · Pre-class reading (Sept 11)

The law under the demo

L02 Pre-class · Step 1 / 8Conservation of Energy

The law, as the slides state it:

  • Each part of a physical system has a certain amount of energy.
  • The total energy of an isolated system doesn't change with time.
  • BUT energy can be transferred between different parts and take different forms.
  • In thermodynamics: the microscopic kinetic and potential energy of atoms and molecules.
Keep precise: isolated (nothing enters or leaves — total locked) and transfer (energy moves between parts and changes form — that's what heat is).

Clicker 1 — Conservation of energy was first fully understood by… A · Newton · B · Einstein · C · Emmy Noether · D · James Joule. Answer deliberately not revealed on a pre-class page — commit to a guess; the instructor resolves it in the lecture.

Connect it back to the brick: L01's "heat flows until equilibrium" silently used this law — what leaves the air is exactly what the brick gains.

L02 Pre-class · Step 2 / 8Heating at the molecular level

  • Heating = adding energy at the molecular level; cooling = removing it.
  • The hot brick's atoms carry more kinetic AND more potential energy than the cold brick's.
Lecture extension: why does a vibration hold potential energy? A vibrating atom pushes against its neighbours — the stored push is that potential energy.

L02 Pre-class · Step 3 / 8Macroscopic properties

  • Most of them change — often only slightly for small ΔT: size, volume, density; IR output at different frequencies; e.g. electrical conductivity.
  • Thermal expansion — typical, with exceptions: solids, gases in balloons, liquids in tubes: hotter → bigger. "Size" = length, area, or volume.
  • Hotter → more IR radiation.

L02 Pre-class · Step 4 / 8Clicker 2 — predict: contact between two blocks

What happens if we put a hot block in contact with a room-temperature block?

A · Nothing   B · Heat flows until they are the same temperature   C · The hot block cools to room temperature   D · The room-temperature block heats to the hot block's temperature   E · None of the above
Answer deliberately not revealed on a pre-class page — the lecture resolves it (see Part IV).

L02 Pre-class · Step 5 / 8The rule of contact: three outcomes

1 · Nothing changes → the systems are in EQUILIBRIUM, SAME TEMPERATURE.
2 · Energy flows brick → balloon = flow of HEAT; brick has the higher temperature.
3 · Same, other direction — balloon hotter.
Course wording confirmed: heat = thermal energy flowing from one object to another because of a temperature difference. Direction set by temperature — never by "who has more energy".

L02 Pre-class · Step 6 / 8The Zeroth Law — why thermometers work

If A is in equilibrium with B, and A is in equilibrium with C, then B and C are in equilibrium with each other. "Otherwise, temperature wouldn't make sense!"

Clicker 3 — A mercury thermometer sits in a glass of water and reads 20 °C. We can conclude: A · water is 20 °C · B · mercury is 20 °C · C · both · D · neither. Answer deliberately not revealed on a pre-class page — the post-class deep-dive unpacks it (Part IV).

L02 Pre-class · Step 7 / 8Demo prediction: the IR camera

  • (a) just as the blocks touch;

No predicted answers on a pre-class page — the activity is preserved for lecture. Hints that ARE on the slides: the camera displays hotter objects as brighter, and it reads infrared emission (the "every object radiates" physics from L01).

L02 Pre-class · Step 8 / 860-second review

  • Conservation of Energy: isolated system's total constant; energy transfers between parts and changes form; thermo tracks microscopic KE and PE.
  • Heating/cooling = adding/removing molecular energy; hot brick = more kinetic and potential energy.
  • Most macroscopic properties shift with temperature — usually slightly. Expansion typical, exceptions exist.
  • Contact = three outcomes; direction set by temperature, never by total energy.
  • Zeroth Law: A–B and A–C in equilibrium ⇒ B–C in equilibrium. Thermometers work because of it.
  • Next class: methods for measuring temperature.

Worked example — the IR demo, resolved (slide 15)

Stage 1 · before contact
Each block in equilibrium with something else: the dark one with the room, the yellow one with the hot plate. Equilibrium is always with respect to something.
Stage 2 · thermal contact
The two-block system is now isolated (ignore table and air). Inside it: a temperature difference exists → heat must flow.
Stage 3 · during the flow
The slide's subtle line: the blocks are not in equilibrium with each other, and each block is not in an equilibrium STATE — some parts are hotter. Equilibrium requires temperature to be uniform inside each object too. This upgrades the ledger's precision item.
Stage 4 · the endpoint
Same temperature AND uniform — net flow zero, microscopic exchanges continue. One uniform brightness on the IR camera.

Variations — asked in the live demo

V1 · Does touching a thermometer lower your body temperature?
Any measurement affects the system — that's the rule. Here the effect is negligible: the thermometer is tiny compared to you.
V2 · Why did the eyes of people wearing glasses look dark on the IR camera?
Glass does not transmit infrared. The eyes radiate like the rest of the face — but their IR can't reach the camera through the lenses. Reading an instrument means knowing what it cannot see.
V3 · Three lamps (LED, CFL, incandescent) all look white — which is the incandescent on the IR photo?
The brightest one: incandescent bulbs waste most electric energy as heat (~5% becomes light) → huge IR output. CFL better; LED most efficient.

Concept library · 14 pages

Every blue term in the interactive version opens one of these

introduced · L01 Microscopic & Macroscopic Description 微观与宏观描述

Two vocabularies for the same object: microscopic (vibrate about fixed sites in solids; translate and collide in gases; radiate) vs macroscopic (the few measurable averages). Chain: microscopic behaviour → averages → macroscopic variables → thermodynamic laws. Mappings: size ↔ spacing; T ↔ avg KE.

introduced · L01 Temperature 温度

A macroscopic variable and a state variable: in the model introduced in L01, temperature reflects the average kinetic energy of a system's particles. Sets the direction of spontaneous heat flow and the endpoint (equilibrium).

introduced · L01 Volume 体积

Macroscopic variable related to the average particle spacing (for a given amount and structure of matter) — why thermal expansion is visible.

introduced · L01 Kinetic Energy 动能

Energy of motion, Ek = ½mv² — background formula, not introduced in L01. Thermal role: temperature reflects the average KE of particles — in the brick, of atoms vibrating about their sites.

introduced · L01 Thermodynamics 热力学

The study of how macroscopic variables summarize ~10²³ particles and the rules those summaries obey.

introduced · L01 Heat 热

Energy in transit driven by ΔT. Carriers: molecular collisions; radiation (IR). ⚠️ An object contains internal energy — it does not contain heat. L02 grounds heat flow in conservation of energy.

introduced · L01 Thermal Equilibrium 热平衡

Endpoint of heat flow: common temperature; net exchange = 0 while two-way collisions continue. L02 adds: equilibrium also requires temperature to be uniform inside each object (slide 15).

previewed · L01 Phase Change 相变

Solid/liquid/gas transitions. L01: vapour → frost on the −78.5 °C brick (deposition, 凝华; slides' shorthand "condensation"). Latent heat comes later.

previewed · L01 Thermal Expansion 热膨胀

L02: every linear dimension grows with temperature — typical behaviour, with exceptions. Quantitative law next.

previewed · L01 Oscillations 振动

Repeated motion about an equilibrium position — already true inside the brick. Second half builds single oscillators → waves.

previewed · L01 Waves 波

Travelling collective motion: energy handed neighbour to neighbour without the material travelling. Wave equation later.

introduced · L01 Electromagnetic Radiation 电磁辐射

Every object emits at any temperature; wavelength set by temperature; room-T → infrared (invisible). L02 adds the spectrum map: gamma (~10⁻¹⁴ m) → AM (~10⁴ m); visible 400–700 nm; hotter → more IR.

introduced · L02 Conservation of Energy 能量守恒

Total energy of an isolated system is constant; energy transfers between parts and changes forms; thermo tracks microscopic KE + PE. In L02 Clicker 1 the instructor keyed Emmy Noether (conservation derived from time symmetry) and used James Joule — the energy unit — as the trap.

introduced · L02 Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics 热力学第零定律

A–B and A–C in equilibrium ⇒ B–C in equilibrium. "Otherwise, temperature wouldn't make sense!" It is what makes thermometers legitimate. Named "zeroth" because it was recognized after the first and second laws already had numbers.
